\section{Related Work}

Friedberg's 1957 paper constructed two recursively enumerable sets of
incomparable degrees and solved Post's problem \cite{friedberg1957}.
Muchnik independently obtained the same solution in 1956, with an expanded
publication appearing later \cite{muchnik1956,muchnik1958}.  Sacks proved
the splitting theorem in 1963 \cite{sacks1963}.  Soare's monograph remains
the standard reference for these constructions and for priority methods
generally \cite{soare1987}.

\paragraph{Synthetic formalizations.}
Post's problem has been mechanized before, and we should be explicit about
it.  Zeng, Forster and Kirst formalize a solution by the priority method in
Coq \cite{zeng2024post}, building on the synthetic oracle computability of
Forster, Kirst and M\"uck \cite{forster2023oracle}.  These developments are
\emph{synthetic}: they work in the Calculus of Inductive Constructions and
take every function $\N \to \N$ to be computable by axiom, so there is no
object-level machine model.  That is a deliberate and effective choice.
The standard complaint about mechanizing computability theory is that
explicit models of computation drown the argument in details that stay
invisible on paper, and synthetic computability removes exactly those
details.

The present development is \emph{analytic}, and the contrast is the main
methodological point of this paper.  There is an explicit language of
oracle programs, a step-indexed evaluator over finite oracle information,
and a use principle that is a proved theorem about that evaluator rather
than an axiom about a computability predicate.  We pay the cost the
synthetic approach avoids.  What is bought is that every appeal to
``preserve the computation below its use'' in the informal proof
corresponds to an application of one lemma about one evaluator, and that
the resulting reducibility relation can be compared with an external one
by a proof rather than by fiat---see below.  Neither approach is superior;
they answer different questions about the same theorem.

\paragraph{Mathlib.}
The development builds on Mathlib's ordinary computability theory.
Carneiro formalized primitive and partial recursive functions in Lean,
including universal partial recursive functions and the halting problem
\cite{carneiro2019}; Mathlib's \texttt{Partrec} and \texttt{PartrecCode}
libraries provide the closure theorems and code infrastructure reused here
\cite{mathlibPartrec,mathlibPartrecCode}.

Mathlib also contains a model of oracle computability
\cite{mathlibTuringDegree}: an inductive relation \texttt{RecursiveIn O f}
on partial functions $\N \rightharpoonup \N$, Turing reducibility and
equivalence, and Turing degrees as the quotient, carrying a
\texttt{PartialOrder} instance.  It does not contain a use principle,
oracle-relative step-indexed evaluation, oracle-relative computable
enumerability, the jump, or any priority argument; and it is not connected
to Mathlib's computably enumerable predicates, which live in a separate
file developed for the unrelativized theory.  The two libraries are
complementary rather than competing: Mathlib has the degree \emph{structure}
we do not build, and we supply the finite-computation machinery it lacks.

We therefore prove the connection rather than asserting it.  A bridge file
establishes that every reduction in Mathlib's \texttt{RecursiveIn} model is
realized by a program of our own language, by induction over
\texttt{RecursiveIn} with one program per constructor.  This is the
direction that matters here: both of our conclusions are \emph{negations}
of reducibility, so the risk to guard against is that our relation admits
too few reductions and thereby makes the negations cheap.  It does not, and
both theorems are consequently restated over Mathlib's relation.  The
converse inclusion is not needed for these results and is not proved.

\paragraph{Other systems.}
The Isabelle Archive of Formal Proofs includes a universal Turing machine
formalization with undecidability material \cite{afpUTM}, on Isabelle/HOL's
infrastructure \cite{nipkow2002}; Coq has been used to formalize abstract
computability through Turing categories \cite{cockett2018}.  These address
foundational models rather than priority arguments.

\paragraph{Priority claims.}
Two claims, stated with the caution they deserve.  We are not aware of
another formalization of Post's problem in an analytic setting with an
explicit oracle machine model, nor of another in Lean; \cite{zeng2024post}
is the synthetic precedent.  We are also not aware of any formalization of
the Sacks Splitting Theorem in any proof assistant.  Both claims rest on a
literature and repository search rather than an exhaustive one, and neither
is load-bearing for the technical content of this paper.
