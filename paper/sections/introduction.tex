\section{Introduction}

Post's problem asks whether there is a computably enumerable set whose
Turing degree is neither computable nor complete.  Friedberg and Muchnik
answered this question independently by constructing two computably
enumerable sets of incomparable degree \cite{friedberg1957,muchnik1956}.
The method introduced in these proofs, now called the priority method, is
one of the defining proof techniques of classical computability theory.

Priority arguments are difficult formalization targets because their
textbook presentation relies on disciplined but informal temporal
reasoning.  A proof says to choose a fresh witness, wait until a computation
converges, preserve the computation by restraining future enumeration below
its use, initialize lower-priority strategies when a stronger strategy
acts, and then argue that higher-priority strategies eventually stop
acting.  In a proof assistant these phrases must become explicit data and
theorems: a state component for witnesses and restraints, a decidable
attention predicate, a deterministic transition function, a preserved
invariant, and an induction proving eventual stability.

This paper describes a Lean 4 formalization of the Friedberg--Muchnik
theorem.  The central contribution is not a new mathematical proof of the
classical theorem, but a machine-checked account of the finite-injury
method in which finite oracle computations, uses, restraints,
initialization, attention, injury, eventual stability, and effective stage
construction are all represented directly.  The development is organized
around a project-local oracle-machine model, with Mathlib used only for
ordinary primitive-recursive and partial-recursive closure obligations.

The final theorem is:

\begin{lstlisting}
theorem friedberg_muchnik :
    exists A B : Set Nat,
      CE A /\ CE B /\ not (A <=T B) /\ not (B <=T A)
\end{lstlisting}

In the actual Lean source the relation is named
\texttt{TuringReducible} and displayed with scoped notation
\texttt{\(\leq\)\textsuperscript{T}}.  The theorem is proved by taking the
two limit sets \texttt{Aset} and \texttt{Bset}, proving them c.e. in
\texttt{CE.lean}, and proving the two nonreducibilities in
\texttt{Requirements.lean}.

The contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item an explicit finite oracle semantics with output and use tracking;
\item a verified agreement-below-use preservation theorem;
\item a complete stage-by-stage formalization of the Friedberg--Muchnik
  priority construction;
\item a finite-injury proof based on a formal rank and induction over
  priority;
\item a computability proof showing that the limiting sets are genuinely
  c.e.; and
\item a final theorem stated in set-theoretic computability vocabulary.
\end{enumerate}

The paper is careful about the scope of the local reducibility relation.
The final theorem proves mutual nonreducibility in the project's
oracle-machine model.  Reflexivity, the effective program numbering, and
basic sanity lemmas are proved, but transitivity of the local reducibility
relation is not currently part of the repository.  We therefore treat the
full internal development of Turing degrees as future work.
